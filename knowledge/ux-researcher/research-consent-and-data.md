# Informed consent and research data handling for user sessions

- Source: Getting informed consent for user research — https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/getting-users-consent-for-research (accessed 2026-11-28)
- Supporting: Managing user research data and participant privacy — https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/managing-user-research-data-participant-privacy (accessed 2026-11-28)
- Publisher: Government Digital Service (UK), GOV.UK Service Manual
- Licence: OGL-UK-3.0 · Copy in repo: link only

Why this matters for ux-researcher: any usability session for this project creates notes, recordings
and quotes about real people. This note covers what to tell participants before a session and how
to keep research files out of the repository.

## Key facts: what to tell participants
- Who is doing the research and why; what data is collected; what happens in the session.
- How results are used and who sees them; whether the session is observed (and by whom) and recorded.
- That taking part is voluntary and they can withdraw at any time.
- How long data is kept; their rights and how to complain; who the data controller is; any other
  organisations processing data (for example transcription).
- Give a plain-language information sheet during recruitment and before the session, in a usable format.

## Key facts: recording consent
- Everyone needs to consent, colleagues included.
- Face to face: signed form, scanned, paper shredded. Remote: emailed or posted form plus a reply
  confirming consent. Verbal consent on the recording is fine if they read the form first.
- Online surveys: information and a positive confirmation step first.
- Keep evidence of each consent with the data it covers; name files by date, round number and
  participant number.
- Disabled participants: offer to read the form aloud, help with signing, a digital version for
  assistive technology, or verbal consent on the recording.
- Children and vulnerable adults: consent from the participant and from a responsible adult.
- Remind people they can stop or withdraw at the start and end of the session, and whenever you are unsure. If they
  withdraw, stop and delete their data.

## Key facts: data handling
- Treat all research data as personal data: notes, photos, audio, video, what people type into
  prototypes, screening answers.
- Collect only what the research questions need.
- Use approved devices and encrypted storage; move files off recorders quickly and wipe them.
- Restrict access to people who need it; prevent copying and downloading where possible.
- Retention: agree a period; the guidance suggests 2 or 3 years, deleting sooner when no longer needed.
- Use fully anonymised extracts (quotes, screenshots, clips) wherever possible; always remove
  names, contact details and confidential details; blur screens or bleep audio.
- It is best not to make non-anonymised outputs public; remove personal data and fully anonymise anything published.
- Brief observers and note-takers on their responsibilities before sessions.

## How to apply it here
- Write a one-page information sheet for the NLP Lab sessions using the list above, in plain language.
- Store recordings and consent records outside the git working copy; the repository holds only
  anonymised findings (P1, P2...), never names, faces or contact details.
- In findings, quote briefly and strip details that could identify someone (job, place, employer).
- File naming for raw notes: `2026-11-28_round1_P3.md`, kept outside the repo.
- Delete raw data when the project ends or the agreed period runs out; record the deletion date.

## Limits
- General guidance for UK government research; it names "data protection law" without legal
  detail. This is not legal advice; legal questions go to the privacy and compliance agent.
- Neither page covers incentives or payment to participants.
- Pseudonymisation is not described beyond anonymising extracts.
