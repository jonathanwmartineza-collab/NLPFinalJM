# Quick usability tests for content (Digital.gov)

- Source: Usability testing (Plain language guide, Test for understanding) — https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/test/usability-testing (accessed 2026-11-28)
- Supporting: Usability testing (Research and collaboration guide) — https://digital.gov/guides/research-collaboration/testing/usability (accessed 2026-11-28)
- Publisher: Digital.gov, U.S. General Services Administration
- Licence: public domain (US federal government work, per Digital.gov policies page; no SPDX id) · Copy in repo: link only

Why this matters for ux-researcher: the cheapest test for this project is whether visitors
understand the NLP Lab's explanations and the chat's guidance. This guide gives a light, repeatable
format for testing words, not only screens.

## Key facts: plain-language usability test
- Test any time you have draft content; after revising, run a second round to confirm the fix and
  check you did not create new problems.
- Recruit three to five people; define the intended readers and simple matching criteria.
- Colleagues, friends and family can take part, but not members of your own team.
- About one hour each; no special permission is needed for a small test.
- Session: introduction (comfort, process, a few questions about relevant experience) > scenarios
  (short stories that suggest a need for information; watch and listen) > debrief (neutral
  questions; follow up on specific words or phrases).
- Scenario example style: one or two sentences giving a situation and a goal.
- Ask people to think aloud; have a note-taker watch and listen.
- Variations: co-discovery (two people together), group sessions (more note-takers), comparative
  tests (alternate which version comes first), remote moderated, remote unmoderated (large samples).
- Case example: one changed title fixed a misunderstanding about who the content was for.

## Key facts: research-collaboration usability test
- Test anything from a sketch or prototype to a competitor's product.
- Steps: choose what to test > plan (scenarios, participants, recruiting, who moderates and who
  observes) > write the script > recruit and get consent > run sessions > discuss results.
- Moderator: gets verbal OK to record, asks for thinking aloud, guides the session.
- Observers: keep a rolling issues log, pass questions to the moderator without interrupting, join a
  debrief after each test.
- End with a shared synthesis meeting that agrees how findings change design decisions.
- Each test takes 20 minutes to 1 hour.

## How to apply it here
- Round 1: 3 to 5 people, 20 to 30 minutes, content-only scenarios, e.g. "A friend says this
  review sounds negative. Use the page to check, then tell me what the score means."
- Ask them to explain results in their own words (sentiment label, entity chips, top bigrams);
  log each misread term against the content designer's glossary.
- Round 2 after rewrites, same scenarios, to confirm the fix.
- Roles: one moderator, one note-taker keeping the issues log; debrief straight after each session.
- Label findings E3 (observed) when behaviour was seen, E2 when it was only said.
- Federal clearance rules for unmoderated tests do not apply to this project; consent still does.

## Limits
- Neither page gives a scoring method for comprehension; paraphrase testing is on a separate page
  not read here.
- Three to five people suits a quick content check of one audience, not measurement.
